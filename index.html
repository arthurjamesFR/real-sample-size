The sample size you really need

Sample sizes are usually calculated by applying the expected treatment effect to the average risk of the population.

But if the treatment lowers each patient's odds of the outcome by the same proportion and patients' risks differ, it prevents fewer events than this calculation assumes, and a trial sized this way is underpowered.

This calculator gives the sample size actually required, with or without adjustment for a risk score, for death or any other yes/no outcome.

Method
Notation
p is each patient's predicted risk of the outcome, p̄ its mean in the population.
Usual calculation
Standard sample-size formula for comparing two proportions (normal approximation), with pC = p̄ in the control arm and pT = the expected effect applied to p̄ in the treated arm:
n per arm = [ z1−α/2 √(2 pm(1 − pm)) + z1−β √(pC(1 − pC) + pT(1 − pT)) ]² / (pC − pT)²
where pm = (pC + pT)/2, z1−α/2 = 1.96 for α = 0.05 and z1−β = 0.84 for 80% power.
Required, unadjusted analysis
Same formula, but the risk in the treated arm is the effect applied to each patient's risk, averaged over patients. With only the mean and SD, it is approximated as the usual calculation × 1/(1 − R²)², where R² = Var(p)/[p̄(1 − p̄)].
Required, adjusted analysis
Logistic regression on treatment and the logit of p, with a large-sample calculation of its variance. The risk score must be available for every patient at randomization.
Assumptions
Traumabase tab: as in the article, TRISS discriminates deaths with an AUC of 0.90. Each patient's risk tab: risks are taken as exact; with a less accurate score, more patients are needed, mainly for the adjusted analysis (about 30% more in the Traumabase with an AUC of 0.90).
Rounding
Required sample sizes are rounded to two significant digits.
Reference: James A, et al. Applying the treatment effect to mean mortality underestimates the sample size of mortality trials: a multicenter trauma registry-based simulation study (manuscript in preparation). This tool supports trial planning and does not replace a statistician's review.
